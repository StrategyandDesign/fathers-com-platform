FILM COURSE · 12 SESSIONS · ~12 MINUTES EACH

Steady Under Pressure

FOR EVERY MAN, ON EITHER TRACK

Twelve short films on steadiness under pressure: notice the surge early, buy six seconds, repair the same day, and build habits that hold.

Self-paced. About twelve minutes each. A Certified Facilitator is available when your seat is claimed.

Start with free ProfileWatch the preview player

Preview is practice only. Earn proof through a Certified Organization.

Important notes

Completion never requires contact with your child. No practice asks you to go beyond the rules that govern you now or any court order. Not anger management, batterer intervention, or a substitute for any court-mandated program. Not designed or validated for those purposes and never ordered in their place.

SESSION 1~12 MIN

The Surge Is a Signal

SESSION FILM

Scratch cut ready in the player. Watch there, then take the checkpoint.

Open in the player

“The surge is a signal, not an order.”

One scene. The noise in the meal line rises, trays and voices. His jaw sets before he knows why. That jaw is the earliest cue, and it is not an order to blow.

In the room. The body's stress response in plain language. You learn that the surge fires before deliberation, and that the skill is what you do in the next few seconds.

What you leave with. You can treat the surge as information instead of a command.

THE PRACTICE

Log three moments of rising tension this week. For each, write the trigger and the earliest body cue you noticed.

The science in the room: The surge chemistry of stress fires before awareness; early cues give you a choice.

Session outlines (2–12)
SESSION 2~12 MIN

Know Your Early Cues

SESSION FILM

Scratch cut ready in the player. Watch there, then take the checkpoint.

Open in the player

“Catch it in the jaw, not the shout.”

One scene. He used to notice only when his voice was already loud. Now he catches the heat in his face and the locked jaw two beats earlier. That gap is the whole game.

In the room. You map your personal early warning signs: jaw, fists, breath, tone, tunnel vision. You pick the two that show up first for you.

What you leave with. You can name your earliest cues before the shout arrives.

THE PRACTICE

Write your top two early cues on a card. Check the card after three hard moments this week.

The science in the room: Body cues precede peak anger; naming them early widens the window to choose.

SESSION 3~12 MIN

Six Seconds

SESSION FILM

Scratch cut ready in the player. Watch there, then take the checkpoint.

Open in the player

“Six seconds buy your judgment back.”

One scene. The words are already loaded. He counts six silent seconds instead. The sentence he almost said stays unsaid, and the one he says next is his.

In the room. What happens in the seconds before a blowup, including the fast pathway that outruns judgment. The tool is a deliberate pause of about six seconds before you speak or act.

What you leave with. You can insert six seconds when you feel the surge.

THE PRACTICE

Practice a six-second pause three times this week in real moments. Record the trigger and what you did after the pause.

The science in the room: A brief pause brings judgment back online after the fast threat pathway fires.

SESSION 4~12 MIN

The Long Exhale

SESSION FILM

Scratch cut ready in the player. Watch there, then take the checkpoint.

Open in the player

“A long exhale stands the body down.”

One scene. His chest is high and tight. He lengthens the out-breath once, slow. The shoulders drop a notch. He can hear himself think again.

In the room. Slow breathing with a longer exhale as a body-down tool you can run anywhere. You pair it with the six-second pause so the skill stacks.

What you leave with. You can lower your own charge on demand with one long exhale.

THE PRACTICE

Run a daily long-exhale drill for five days. Use it once live under pressure and note what changed.

The science in the room: A long slow exhale signals the body to stand down faster than willpower alone.

SESSION 5~12 MIN

Step Away to Come Back

SESSION FILM

Scratch cut ready in the player. Watch there, then take the checkpoint.

Open in the player

“Step away to come back.”

One scene. Mid-argument on a call, his voice is climbing. He says he needs ten minutes and that he is coming back. He does, on time, quieter. The coming back is the skill.

In the room. Using a time-out on yourself as a reset, not a punishment. You practice leaving without abandoning the person or the topic.

What you leave with. You can step away to cool down and return on time.

THE PRACTICE

Take one clean self time-out during a hard moment where you are now, then return when you said. Report both ends.

The science in the room: A clean break lets the body reset; the return keeps it from becoming abandonment.

SESSION 6~12 MIN

The Line You Leave On

SESSION FILM

Scratch cut ready in the player. Watch there, then take the checkpoint.

Open in the player

“Say the line, then leave the room.”

One scene. He almost storms out silent. Instead he says: I need ten minutes. I am coming back. Then he leaves. The room stays safer because the line held the door open.

In the room. You write and rehearse a short leave-line that names the break and the return time. No speech. No blame. Just the line, then the exit.

What you leave with. You have a leave-line ready so a break is a reset, not a disappearance.

THE PRACTICE

Write your leave-line in under fifteen words. Rehearse it aloud three times. Use it once this week if needed.

The science in the room: Naming the return turns a break into a trustworthy pause.

SESSION 7~12 MIN

Name the Feeling

SESSION FILM

Scratch cut ready in the player. Watch there, then take the checkpoint.

Open in the player

“Say the feeling so you do not have to show it.”

One scene. Instead of the slammed cabinet, he says I am frustrated, and not at you. Five words. The temperature drops a degree, and the child hears the words too.

In the room. Putting feelings into words lowers their intensity. You practice naming your own state in plain language before it becomes a show.

What you leave with. You can handle feelings by naming them instead of acting them out.

THE PRACTICE

Name one feeling out loud in three separate moments this week. Log the words you used.

The science in the room: Naming a feeling out loud measurably lowers its charge.

SESSION 8~12 MIN

Feelings Without Weapons

SESSION FILM

Scratch cut ready in the player. Watch there, then take the checkpoint.

Open in the player

“Name it without loading it.”

One scene. He almost says you always do this. He swaps it for I am angry and I want to finish this calmly. Same feeling. No weapon attached.

In the room. You strip blame, insults, and history dumps from the naming sentence. Clean naming lowers heat; loaded naming restarts the fight.

What you leave with. You can name hard feelings without arming them.

THE PRACTICE

Rewrite two loaded sentences into clean feeling statements. Use one clean line live this week.

The science in the room: Blame reloads conflict; clean labeling keeps the skill usable.

SESSION 9~12 MIN

Own It Same Day

SESSION FILM

Scratch cut ready in the player. Watch there, then take the checkpoint.

Open in the player

“Own it out loud, same day.”

One scene. Last night he barked over nothing. This morning, before anything else: I was wrong to raise my voice. You did not deserve that. Same day. No wait.

In the room. Repair after a rupture protects the relationship. You practice owning the moment out loud the same day, specific and without excuses.

What you leave with. You can own a bad moment the same day it happened.

THE PRACTICE

Write one ownership line for a recent miss. Deliver it same day if contact is allowed, or deliver a live ownership to someone where you are now.

The science in the room: Fast ownership keeps a rupture from hardening into the relationship's normal.

SESSION 10~12 MIN

The Short Apology

SESSION FILM

Scratch cut ready in the player. Watch there, then take the checkpoint.

Open in the player

“Short, specific, no defense.”

One scene. He starts to explain the week. He stops. He says only: I raised my voice at you. That was wrong. I will step away next time. Ten words. It lands.

In the room. You practice a short apology script: the specific act, that it was wrong, and one next step. Defense stays out.

What you leave with. You can apologize short, specific, and without defense.

THE PRACTICE

Write a short apology for one real miss. Cut every excuse. Rehearse once aloud, then deliver if allowed.

The science in the room: Short specific repair lands; defense undoes it.

SESSION 11~12 MIN

Sleep, Food, Movement

SESSION FILM

Scratch cut ready in the player. Watch there, then take the checkpoint.

Open in the player

“Steadiness is built in the boring hours.”

One scene. Lights out at the same hour five nights straight, a walk or a workout most days, and by day five the fuse is longer. He can see it in his own log.

In the room. The daily regulators that raise your threshold: sleep, food, and movement. Willpower sits on top of a body that is fed and rested.

What you leave with. You can lower baseline reactivity through habits practicable anywhere.

THE PRACTICE

Hold one sleep anchor and one movement block for five days. Note food timing on the same log.

The science in the room: Sleep, food, and movement raise the threshold where steadiness breaks.

SESSION 12~12 MIN

Your Steady Week

SESSION FILM

Scratch cut ready in the player. Watch there, then take the checkpoint.

Open in the player

“Stack the small things until they hold.”

One scene. Nothing dramatic happened this week. He ran the pause, the leave-line, the short apology, and the sleep anchor. That is what holding looks like from inside.

In the room. You fold the course into a one-page steady week: cues, six seconds, exhale, leave-line, same-day ownership, and two boring-hour anchors.

What you leave with. You leave with a written steady-week plan and one measurable marker.

THE PRACTICE

Complete a one-page steady-week plan with one weekly marker you will report to your facilitator or accountability partner.

The science in the room: Small repeated anchors outperform rare heroic effort.
